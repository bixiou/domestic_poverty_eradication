Dear VoxDev Editors,

I would like to submit for your consideration my research on the domestic capacity of developing countries to eradicate extreme poverty by 2030.

**The paper.** "Shortfall of Domestic Resources to Eradicate Extreme Poverty by 2030" was published in the *Journal of Development Studies* in 2025 (doi:10.1080/00220388.2025.2493807). I have just updated its results with the World Bank's September 2026 poverty data, which use the 2021 PPPs, the new $3.00 international poverty line and recent surveys for most low-income countries. The update note is attached; it has been submitted to *Economics Bulletin*. Code and data are public: github.com/bixiou/domestic_poverty_eradication.

**The question.** With four years left before the 2030 deadline of SDG 1, can countries end extreme poverty with their own resources, through growth and domestic redistribution?

**Main findings (updated data).**
- Growth alone will not suffice: after the growth observed up to 2025, and with 3% annual growth thereafter, 7.6% of the world population would remain below $3.00 a day in 2030, and 5.2% even with 7% growth.
- Even radical redistribution falls short in the poorest countries: in eleven countries, mostly low-income countries of Sub-Saharan Africa, taxing all consumption above $8.30 a day at 100% would not raise enough to lift everyone above $3.00 a day. A more realistic 10% tax would fall short in 30 countries.
- The findings are robust to the data revision: at the price-equivalent of the former $2.15 line, results are very close to the original ones. India stands out, as its new surveys show it can end extreme poverty with modest redistribution.
- Even by 2035, eight low-income countries would still lack the resources to end extreme poverty at $3.00 a day.
- Globally, the cost is small: closing the extreme poverty gap would take 0.31% of world income, e.g. a 2.7% tax on consumption above $100 a day. In absolute terms, this is about $112 billion a year, i.e. 64% of all official development assistance in 2025, while the gap in Sub-Saharan Africa ($90 billion) is three times the bilateral aid the region received.
- Only a decade of 7% annual growth from 2025 would allow every country to end extreme poverty by capping incomes at $8.30 a day by 2035.

**Policy relevance.** The results distinguish countries where domestic resource mobilization can end extreme poverty from those where international transfers are indispensable, at a time when aid budgets are being cut and global taxation of top wealth is discussed at the G20. They are timely given the release of the World Bank's *Poverty, Prosperity, and Planet Report 2026*.

If the Editorial Board finds it suitable, I would be happy to write a column. I have a map ready showing, country by country, the redistribution required, and an interactive website with all country data.
